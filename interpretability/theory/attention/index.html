Step 1 of 6

A weighted average of what you have seen

You have pairs (xi, yi) and a new point x. The oldest non-parametric answer to "what is y at x?" is to average the yi, weighting each by how much its xi resembles x:

f̂(x) = Σi K(x, xi) yiΣj K(x, xj)

K is a kernel: any non-negative function that is large when its two arguments are close. The usual choice is Gaussian, K(x, xi) = exp(−‖x − xi‖2/2). The denominator makes the weights sum to one, so the estimate is a convex combination of the yi: it never leaves the range of what it has seen.

Nadaraya, E. A. (1964). On estimating regression. Theory of Probability and Its Applications 9(1), 141-142. Watson, G. S. (1964). Smooth regression analysis. Sankhyā A 26(4), 359-372.

Step 2 of 6

Change the kernel

Expand the Gaussian: ‖x − xi‖2 = ‖x‖2 − 2 x·xi + ‖xi‖2. The ‖x‖2 term is the same for every i and cancels between numerator and denominator. What is left depends on the data only through an inner product. So take the inner product as the kernel itself, and let the two sides be different linear views of the inputs:

K(x, xi) = exp( q · ki / √d ),   q = WQ x,   ki = WK xi

The √d keeps the exponent from growing with the dimension. Nothing else about the estimator has changed: it is still a kernel-weighted average, with a kernel the model can learn instead of one you fixed in advance.

This is the reading in Tsai, Bai, Yamada, Morency, Salakhutdinov (2019). Transformer Dissection: A Unified Understanding of Transformer's Attention via the Lens of Kernel. EMNLP. The textbook version of the same derivation is Dive into Deep Learning, chapter 11, Attention Pooling by Similarity.

Step 3 of 6

The normalised weights are a softmax

Put the new kernel back into the Nadaraya-Watson ratio. The weight on the i-th point is

αi = exp(q · ki / √d)Σj exp(q · kj / √d) = softmax(qkT/√d)i

and the estimate is f̂(x) = Σi αi vi with vi = WV xi in place of yi. Written for all positions at once this is softmax(QKT/√d) V, the attention of Vaswani et al. (2017). In a language model the xi are the earlier tokens' residual-stream vectors and x is the current one; the causal mask just restricts the sum to i ≤ current position.

Vaswani, Shazeer, Parmar, Uszkoreit, Jones, Gomez, Kaiser, Polosukhin (2017). Attention Is All You Need. NeurIPS.

Step 4 of 6

The kernel is QK. The values are OV.

Look at what each weight matrix touches. The weights αi depend on WQ and WK only through the product WQT WK: one bilinear form that scores every (current token, earlier token) pair. That is the QK circuit. It answers where to look.

What actually gets added to the residual stream is WO Σi αi WV xi = Σi αi (WOWV) xi. The matrix WOWV is the OV circuit. It answers what to move, and it never sees the weights. In the kernel picture: QK is the kernel, OV is the yi.

head(x) = Σi softmax(xT WQTWK xi / √d)  ·  WOWV xi

Two independent linear maps per head, and neither one alone tells you what the head does. The framework paper draws this split and names it; everything on the poster's left-hand side is the QK half, everything in the attribution graph is the OV half plus the MLPs.

Elhage, Nanda, Olsson et al. (2021). A Mathematical Framework for Transformer Circuits. Anthropic.

Step 5 of 6

An induction head is a kernel with one sharp peak

Suppose the sequence contains [A][B] somewhere and the current token is [A] again. An induction head's QK circuit puts almost all of α on one position: the token right after the previous [A]. (It can do this because an earlier "previous-token" head has already written "I follow an A" into [B]'s residual stream.) Its OV circuit then copies that token's identity forward, so the prediction is [B].

In the kernel language: a kernel that is nearly zero everywhere except at "the position after the last time I saw this token", and values that are just the token identity. Olsson et al. argue this pair of heads is the main mechanism behind in-context learning.

A local variable that appears three times inside a function is this pattern exactly. The return position asks "what usually follows the thing I am looking at?", and the names that appeared earlier are the candidates. The poster's attention heatmap is a picture of that kernel, per head, from the return position.

Olsson, Elhage, Nanda et al. (2022). In-context Learning and Induction Heads. Anthropic.

Step 6 of 6

Back to the poster: where, what, does it matter

The kernel view is why the poster splits the question in three. The attention pattern is the kernel: it says where the return position looks, and nothing more. What is moved along those weights, through OV and through the MLPs, is what the attribution graph shows, with every effect named against a logit. And whether a pathway matters is a separate question again, answered by scaling it down and watching the prediction change.

This is also why an attention heatmap on its own is not an explanation: high weight on a token says the kernel is peaked there, not that what was moved decided the answer. Jain and Wallace (2019) make the point at length. On the poster the heatmap never stands alone; it sits beside the graph and the dose-response curve.
